# Latest Research Findings (2023-2025) on Endurance Cycling Training

## Block Training With Moderate‐ or High‐Intensity Intervals (Rønnestad et al., 2025)

### Study Design
- Compared 1-week blocks of moderate-intensity interval training (MIT) vs. high-intensity interval training (HIT) in well-trained cyclists
- Participants: 22 cyclists (21 male, 1 female) with VO₂max = 69.5 ± 6.0 mL·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹
- MIT block: 6 sessions over 7 days (5-7 × 10-14-min work intervals, RPE: 14.5)
- HIT block: 5 sessions over 6 days (5 × 8.75-min with multiple short intervals, RPE: 17.1)
- Post-testing after 6 days of active recovery

### Key Findings
- Both MIT and HIT blocks improved 15-min maximal cycling power:
  - MIT: 4.9% improvement
  - HIT: 2.8% improvement
  - No significant difference between approaches (p = 0.44)
- MIT showed greater improvement in power at 4mmol/L lactate (threshold power):
  - MIT: 4.5% improvement
  - HIT: 2.1% improvement
  - Significant difference (p = 0.03, moderate effect size)
- HIT showed trend toward better sprint power improvement:
  - HIT: 1.5% improvement
  - MIT: -1.5% decrease
  - Not statistically significant (p = 0.08)
- No differences in VO₂max changes between approaches
- MIT induced larger increase in % of VO₂max at lactate threshold compared to HIT

### Practical Implications
- Both short blocks (MIT and HIT) can effectively improve endurance performance
- Training adaptations appear to be intensity-specific:
  - MIT (lower intensity, longer intervals) better for threshold improvements
  - HIT (higher intensity, shorter intervals) potentially better for sprint power
- Block training approach (concentrated load followed by recovery) effective for well-trained cyclists
- Coaches can select MIT or HIT blocks based on specific performance goals and limiters

## Recent Advances in Training Intensity Distribution Theory (Sun et al., 2025)

### Key Finding
- Pyramidal training distribution (most time in low intensity, less in moderate, least in high) can comprehensively improve physical functions of elite endurance athletes
- This updates previous emphasis on polarized training (high volume of low intensity + small amount of high intensity)

## Heavy Strength Training Effects (Llanos-Lagos et al., 2025)

### Key Finding
- Heavy strength training can improve cycling performance (time to exhaustion and time trial) in endurance cyclists
- Reinforces the importance of strength training component in endurance cycling programs

## VO₂Max Gains Insights (Athletica.ai, 2025)

### Key Finding
- Study on trained cyclists revealed that those who responded best to high-intensity workouts had a strong foundation of low-intensity training
- Supports the importance of building aerobic base before emphasizing high-intensity work

## Exercise "Trains" the Immune System (SciTechDaily, 2025)

### Key Finding
- Immune cells of older adults with history of endurance training are more effective
- Suggests endurance training provides additional health benefits beyond performance

## Predicting Cycling Performance (Vos et al., 2025)

### Key Finding
- Machine learning models can predict 4-km cycling performance before and after a 12-week intervention
- Potential application for more individualized training prescription based on physiological markers

## Short-term Cycling Interventions in Older Adults (Citherlet et al., 2025)

### Key Finding
- Improved exercise-related skeletal muscle oxygen consumption following endurance training
- Measured using near-infrared spectroscopy
- Relevant for masters athletes and health applications of cycling

## Ultra-endurance Race Performance and Muscle Mass (Vilite et al., 2025)

### Study Design
- Examined 43 recreational cyclists completing a 341 km ultra-endurance race over 18 hours
- Cognitive function assessed using three-stage Stroop Test before, immediately after, and 24h post-race
- Analyzed relationships between cognitive performance and various physiological parameters

### Key Findings
- Higher average race speed associated with improved simple reaction time
- Longer race duration predicted better performance in more cognitively demanding tasks (inhibitory control)
- Muscle mass was the only anthropometric variable significantly linked to enhanced cognition
- Age, heart rate reserve percentage, training volume, fat mass, lean mass, bone mass, and metabolic markers showed no significant associations

### Practical Implications
- Race pacing strategies may influence cognitive resilience during prolonged exertion
- Maintaining muscle mass appears important for cognitive function during endurance events
- Suggests potential cognitive benefits of endurance training beyond physical performance
- Highlights importance of pacing strategies for maintaining cognitive function during long events
